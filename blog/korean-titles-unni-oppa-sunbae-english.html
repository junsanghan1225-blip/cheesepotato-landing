Korean Titles in Real Life: Unni, Oppa, Sunbae, and Why 'You' Is Dangerous

  • Real Korean
  • English

Imagine walking into a bustling Seoul restaurant on a Friday night. You want to ask for more water, so you remember what your introductory textbook taught you: you flip to the pronoun chapter, look directly at the server, and politely say, 「당신, 물 좀 주세요.」 Instantly, the friendly room freezes. The server looks baffled and slightly offended, your Korean friend covers their face in embarrassment, and you wonder what possibly went wrong.

In English, the word 'you' is universal. It works for your best friend, your boss, the bus driver, and the barista. In Korean, however, there is virtually no safe, all-purpose equivalent of 'you'. The literal dictionary translations like 「당신」 or 「너」 are either intensely intimate or aggressively confrontational. Instead of using pronouns, Korean relies on a rich, relational system of social titles (호칭). Here is how real Koreans navigate titles in daily life.

Textbook phrasing versus real-life titles

당신은 오늘 무엇을 먹고 싶습니까?Textbook: What do you want to eat today? (Sounds confrontational or melodramatic)
민수 씨, 오늘 뭐 먹고 싶어요?Real Korean: Minsoo, what do you want to eat today? (Use their name plus 씨)
아주머니, 여기 물 좀 가져다주십시오.Textbook: Middle-aged woman, please bring water here. (Risk of offending the server)
사장님, 여기 물 좀 더 주세요~Real Korean: Boss/manager, could we get some more water here? (Friendly, respectful, and safe)
저의 선생님께 질문이 있습니다.Textbook: I have a question for my honorable teacher. (Stiff and bookish in casual settings)
쌤, 이거 질문 하나만 드려도 돼요?Real Korean: Teacher, can I ask a quick question about this? (Natural spoken contraction)

Cheese and Potato: The restaurant encounter

To understand how jarring textbook titles sound in real conversations, watch what happens when Cheese (🧀) takes Potato (🥔) out for grilled pork belly (삼겹살) using rigid vocabulary memorized from an old grammar guide.

🧀당신, 이 고깃집의 대표 메뉴는 무엇입니까?
🥔으악, 치즈야! 나한테 '당신'이라고 부르지 마. 싸우자는 줄 알았잖아.
🧀어? 교과서에서 'you'가 '당신'이라고 배웠는데요?
🥔한국에서 '당신'은 부부끼리 부르거나 길거리에서 싸울 때만 쓰는 말이야. 그냥 '감자야'나 '형'이라고 불러.

Once Potato explains the social rules, Cheese switches to authentic titles used by 20- and 30-something Koreans. Notice how the tone immediately transforms from stiff confrontation to warm camaraderie.

🧀감자 형, 삼겹살 2인분 먼저 시킬까?
🥔그래, 좋아! 저기 이모님이나 사장님 부르면 돼.
🧀사장님, 여기 삼겹살 2인분하고 된장찌개 하나 주세요!
🥔완벽해! 한국 사람처럼 주문 아주 잘하네.

The family titles: Unni, Oppa, Nuna, and Hyeong

International K-pop and drama fans frequently know the words 오빠 (oppa), 언니 (unni), 누나 (nuna), and 형 (hyeong). In literal terms, these represent older sibling terms: a female calls an older female 「언니」 and an older male 「오빠」, while a male calls an older male 「형」 and an older female 「누나」.

However, using them in real life requires mutual closeness. Calling an older male coworker 「오빠」 in an office setting or addressing a stranger on the subway as 「오빠」 sounds bizarrely flirtatious or unprofessional. You only adopt these sibling titles after you have established a personal friendship outside work and the older person explicitly invites you to use them.

Workplace etiquette: The critical danger of 'Surname + 씨'

Many beginners assume that adding the polite suffix 씨 (ssi) works anywhere. However, placement matters tremendously. Attaching 씨 to someone's given name or full name—such as 「민수 씨」 or 「김민수 씨」—is polite and common among adult colleagues of similar corporate standing.

Never attach 씨 to a surname alone! Calling someone 「김 씨」 (Kim-ssi) or 「박 씨」 (Park-ssi) is deeply condescending in modern Korean; it sounds like an aristocrat addressing an indentured servant or a foreman barking orders at casual manual laborers. In professional companies, the golden formula is always Surname + Job Title + 님, such as 「김 대리님」 (Assistant Manager Kim), 「이 과장님」 (Manager Lee), or 「박 팀장님」 (Team Leader Park).

School and customer titles: Sunbae, Sajangnim, and Ssaem

In university circles or career clubs, senior classmates are addressed as 선배 (sunbae) or 선배님 (sunbaenim). Interestingly, you rarely call someone 후배 (hubae, junior) to their face; juniors are simply called by their given name, such as 「치즈야」 or 「치즈 씨」.

When shopping, dining, or riding taxis, the universal magic word is 사장님 (sajangnim). While it literally means 'company president,' Koreans use it for cafe baristas, convenience store owners, and market vendors. While older generations used 아줌마 (ajumma) or 이모 (imo), modern 20s and 30s avoid 「아줌마」 because it can inadvertently offend younger female proprietors. Sticking with 「사장님」 is always polite and flattering.

Finally, whenever you interact with an instructor—whether a school teacher, your pilates instructor, a personal trainer at the gym, or your hair stylist—the go-to colloquial title is 쌤 (ssaem). It is a cheerful, affectionate contraction of 선생님 (seonsaengnim) that conveys deep respect without feeling stiff or ancient.

Asking someone's name without sounding rude

Early textbooks often feature the awkward question, 「당신의 이름은 무엇입니까?」 Nobody in Seoul says this. If you are meeting someone at a gathering or business lunch and need to clarify how to refer to them, use one of these smooth native alternatives:

In polite social settings, ask 성함이 어떻게 되세요? (formal) or 이름이 어떻게 되세요? (friendly polite). If you are uncertain which social title or honorific level they prefer, say 혹시 어떻게 불러드리면 될까요? ('How should I comfortably address you?'). This gentle inquiry lets the other person choose between their name, job title, or casual sibling term.

CarefulNever use 「당신」 with strangers, superiors, or colleagues. While poetic songs and soap operas feature married couples saying 「당신」 lovingly, using it in daily conversation sounds like an accusation: 'Hey you, what do you think you are doing?' In office environments, always use the person's name plus their job title (e.g. 김 대리님, 이 과장님) or given name plus 씨 (e.g. 지민 씨).
